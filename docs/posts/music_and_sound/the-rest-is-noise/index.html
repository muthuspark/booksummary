The Rest Is Noise by Alex Ross

  • #Music and Sound

Alex Ross · 8 min read

Alex Ross traces twentieth-century classical music through experiments in sound, political terror, mass culture, and the search for new audiences.

Hearing the Twentieth Century Through Its Music

Why did classical composers move from rich orchestral melodies toward harsh chords, electronic sounds, silence, and repeating patterns? In The Rest Is Noise: Listening to the Twentieth Century, Alex Ross rejects a simple story of musical decline. He also resists the idea that each new style necessarily improved on the last.

His central subject is the changing relationship between composers and their societies. Wars, dictatorships, migration, audiences, and technology helped shape what musicians could create. Composers responded in different ways. Some sought a wider public. Others protected a private language or challenged the habits of listening itself. Ross’s account of the book presents these sounds as part of modern history, with connections extending into film, jazz, rock, and dance music.

An Old Musical World Opens from Within

Ross begins with Richard Strauss’s Salome in Graz in 1906. This was not the opera’s first performance. It was a gathering where major musical figures heard an already notorious work. Its disturbing subject and clashes of harmony tested the limits of respectable culture. Yet audiences welcomed it, and Strauss enjoyed commercial success.

This opening complicates the familiar opposition between experiment and popularity. A work could be shocking and successful at once. Strauss and Gustav Mahler inherited the large Romantic orchestra, but used it to expose conflict, uncertainty, and fractured experience. The old musical language already contained forces that could pull it apart. The publisher’s opening excerpt shows Ross moving between musical detail, personalities, and the social world around a performance.

There Was More Than One Route into Modern Music

Much Western classical music relies on tonality: a sense that notes and chords belong around a central key. Even when a passage wanders, listeners can feel its pull toward home. Atonal music weakens or avoids that center. It can still have recurring ideas, careful structure, and emotional direction.

Arnold Schoenberg’s work moved rapidly from Romantic richness toward atonality. Claude Debussy opened different possibilities through ambiguous harmonies and unusual scales. His music could feel suspended rather than driven toward an expected resolution. Ross’s guide to these composers lets readers hear the differences directly.

The important point is that modernism was not one sound. Unfamiliar music might unsettle through violent intensity, extreme brevity, or a calm refusal to arrive where the listener expects.

Folk Traditions Could Produce Radical Change

New music did not always require leaving older cultures behind. Béla Bartók collected village songs and studied their rhythms and melodic shapes. These encounters transformed his own musical language. Folk music became a source of structural discovery, rather than merely a tune to decorate an orchestral score.

Igor Stravinsky’s The Rite of Spring offers another path. Its force comes partly from displaced accents, overlapping patterns, and short rhythmic units of changing length. Try to keep a steady beat while the emphases move unexpectedly. The tension becomes physical. Ross’s chapter guide separates these effects so they can be heard.

This makes the usual contrast between tradition and experiment less useful. A composer could draw on old material to create an unfamiliar experience of time.

Sibelius Challenges a Single Definition of Progress

Jean Sibelius provides a crucial counterexample to the idea that innovation required abandoning tonality. Ross examines how Sibelius changed symphonic form from within. In the Fifth Symphony, musical ideas gradually take shape through repeated transformations. The structure can feel as though it is growing while we listen.

Sibelius also carried the burden of being a national symbol. His reputation varied sharply between countries and critical circles. Ross connects that pressure with the composer’s isolation and struggle to finish new work, without reducing every musical choice to biography.

The lesson of this chapter excerpt is that familiar harmonies can support unusual forms. A work’s position in a fashionable movement tells us less than careful attention to what it does.

Dictatorships Turned Taste into a Matter of Survival

The book’s political center examines music in Stalin’s Russia, Roosevelt’s America, and Hitler’s Germany. These settings reveal different relationships between public support, cultural ambition, and state power. They should not be treated as equivalent systems.

In the Soviet Union, official judgment could change an artist’s prospects overnight. Shostakovich’s opera Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District initially succeeded. A Pravda denunciation followed Stalin’s attendance in 1936. Musical criticism became a threat backed by political authority.

Ross’s Shostakovich guide traces the expressive force of the symphonies that surround this crisis. Their grief, aggression, and apparent triumph invite interpretation. But listeners should distinguish an audible effect from proof of a precise secret message. Under coercion, public conformity and private feeling need not align neatly.

Nazi Germany exposes another disturbing relationship between culture and violence. Reverence for classical masterpieces offered no safeguard against persecution. Ross documents the political use of Beethoven and Wagner, as well as the forced use of musical performance in propaganda. The Theresienstadt film included composer Pavel Haas, who was later murdered at Auschwitz.

Nor did Nazi approval follow one consistent musical rule. Some works influenced by modern styles won favor. Ross’s account of music under Hitler shows why aesthetic taste cannot serve as a reliable measure of moral character.

After the War, Composers Reconsidered Control

Postwar experiment produced strikingly different answers to the question of how music should be organized. Pierre Boulez pursued serial methods that ordered several features of sound, including pitch, duration, and loudness. The attraction was a new degree of formal control.

John Cage explored chance, unconventional instruments, and the limits of the composer’s authority. His prepared piano changed the instrument’s sound through objects placed among its strings. Tape and electronic techniques widened the field further. Pierre Schaeffer assembled recorded noises, including train sounds, as musical material.

Ross places these experiments within the postwar and Cold War world. The contrast is useful: one composer could seek renewal through tighter organization, another through surrendering decisions. Both challenged inherited expectations about what a musical work should be.

Drama, Faith, and Nature Remained Creative Forces

The century did not belong entirely to systems and technical experiments. Benjamin Britten sustained musical drama centered on vulnerable people and threatening communities. In Peter Grimes, the sea, gossip, and collective hostility become powerful elements of the score. Ross’s Britten guide follows the movement between private distress and public pressure.

Olivier Messiaen’s music draws on Catholic devotion and birdsong. Extended songs of praise can alter the listener’s sense of time. His musical recreations of birds connect close attention to nature with a highly individual sound world. The Messiaen chapter guide makes these sources audible. Spiritual commitment and advanced musical technique could work together.

Minimalism Made Repetition a Source of Discovery

The later American story includes composers who built music from sustained sounds, a steady pulse, and repeating patterns. In Steve Reich’s phasing technique, two matching patterns gradually move out of alignment. New rhythmic relationships emerge from that small change.

Reich first explored this process with tape and later used it with instruments. Terry Riley and Philip Glass developed other forms of repetition. Their music invites attention to changes that a listener might miss while waiting for a conventional climax.

Ross’s minimalism guide also traces exchanges with jazz and popular music. Influence moved in several directions. By the final chapter, composers such as Tōru Takemitsu and Tan Dun further complicate any account centered on one European tradition. The closing survey presents multiple musical languages continuing alongside one another.

What This History Changes for a Listener

A practical application of Ross’s approach is to listen for one feature at a time. Ask where the beat falls in Stravinsky. Notice whether Debussy’s harmony seems to settle. Follow the changing relationship between repeated patterns in Reich. These are suggestions drawn from the book’s examples, rather than a formal method Ross prescribes.

Then add context. Who supported the composer? Who could prevent a performance? What did the audience expect? Keep these questions beside close listening. A political history can explain pressures around a score without determining everything the score means.

The Book’s Scope and Limits

This is a selective narrative, first published in 2007, rather than a complete world history of twentieth-century music. Classical composition remains its center. Popular and non-Western traditions receive less sustained treatment. Women also occupy much less space than the major male composers.

Ross acknowledges in his FAQ that narrative choices led him to omit or shorten important careers. Readability comes at the cost of coverage. A compelling sequence of lives can also make historical connections seem more settled than they are. Context and artistic intention remain different kinds of evidence.

The book’s lasting value is its invitation to hear unfamiliar work with patience. Understanding need not begin with agreement about beauty. It can begin with noticing how a piece organizes sound and asking what made that particular musical choice possible.
