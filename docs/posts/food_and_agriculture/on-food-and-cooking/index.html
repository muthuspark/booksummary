On Food and Cooking by Harold McGee

  • #Food and Agriculture

Harold McGee · 8 min read

Understand how heat, proteins, starch, microbes, and browning transform ingredients—and use that knowledge to cook with better judgment.

Understanding What Cooking Changes

An egg turns solid. A tough piece of meat becomes tender. A thin mixture becomes mayonnaise. These familiar changes are the starting point of Harold McGee’s On Food and Cooking: The Science and Lore of the Kitchen. He explains what ingredients contain, how they developed, and what happens when people prepare them.

The book’s central premise is that understanding food makes cooking more intelligible. A recipe tells you what to do. Knowledge of the material helps explain why the instruction works and what might happen if you change it. Science becomes a tool for judgment, alongside taste and experience.

First published in 1984, the book was extensively revised in 2004. This summary follows that revised edition. It is a reference work organized largely around ingredients, from dairy and eggs to plants, grains, sauces, and drinks. McGee connects their chemistry with their history and production. The publisher’s introduction and excerpt establish this approach.

Heat Changes Structure, Not Just Temperature

Cooking involves several changes at once. Heat travels into food. Water moves or evaporates. Molecules change shape or react with each other. The cook must manage their combined effects.

McGee’s discussion of eggs makes this visible. A hard-cooked egg needs enough heat to set its center. Yet its outside heats first. Aggressive boiling can make the outer white rubbery while the yolk finishes cooking. Turbulent water can also crack the shell. In his section on eggs cooked in the shell, he explains the value of gentler heat.

The general lesson is to distinguish the temperature of the surroundings from the state of the food. A hotter pan or more vigorous boil does not improve every result. Sometimes it widens the difference between an overcooked surface and an unfinished center.

Proteins Explain Both Setting and Toughening

Proteins are long molecular chains with particular shapes. Heat can unfold them. This change is called denaturation. Unfolded proteins can then join into a network, a process called coagulation. The network helps turn liquid egg into a tender solid or gives a custard its body.

Continued heating can make that network too tight. It squeezes out water, and a smooth preparation becomes firm, grainy, or watery. Thus the same process can produce both a successful dish and its failure. The American Egg Board’s technical explanation corroborates this mechanism.

McGee also shows why all protein-rich foods do not behave alike. Milk contains proteins with different structures from those in eggs. Its response to heat also depends on acidity and its other components. A useful implication is to avoid treating “protein” as a single ingredient with one cooking rule. The surrounding mixture matters.

Meat Presents Two Competing Goals

Meat makes the problem more complex. Cooking firms muscle proteins and encourages moisture loss. It can also turn tough connective-tissue collagen into gelatin. These changes have different effects on eating quality.

McGee argues that a cooking method must suit the cut. A naturally tender cut does not need the prolonged treatment that helps a collagen-rich cut. In a braise, the connective tissue gradually softens. The muscle fibers themselves can become dry, yet separate more easily. Gelatin contributes its own sense of succulence. Falling-apart tenderness therefore does not mean that the muscle retained all its original water.

His sections on matching cooking to meat texture and collagen becoming gelatin explain this tension. The practical question is what needs to change in this particular cut. “Cook it longer” can either solve a problem or create one.

Browning Builds a Different Kind of Flavor

The flavor of browned food comes partly from new molecules formed during cooking. Maillard reactions involve reactive sugars and amino groups from amino acids and related compounds. They help produce the aromas associated with roasted meat, bread crust, and other browned foods. Caramelization is a separate set of changes involving sugars.

Water helps explain why a simmered ingredient tastes different from a fried one. A wet surface must lose moisture before it can become much hotter than boiling water under ordinary conditions. A dry surface can reach temperatures that produce browning rapidly. McGee’s account of surface browning connects that change with aroma.

For a stew, browning ingredients before adding liquid can create flavors that simmering alone will not produce quickly. This is a choice about the desired flavor. A pale preparation can preserve a different character. Nor is dryness an absolute requirement: McGee describes slow browning in moist foods, where time, concentration, and acidity or alkalinity alter the outcome.

Starch and Gluten Perform Different Jobs

Starch consists of carbohydrate molecules packed into granules. With sufficient water and heat, these granules swell and soften as their internal arrangement loosens. This process helps explain cooked grains and starch-thickened liquids. It is commonly called gelatinization, although it has nothing to do with animal gelatin. McGee makes this distinction in his explanation of starch cooking.

Wheat dough also depends on gluten proteins. Water and mixing allow them to form a connected network. Kneading stretches and organizes that network. A bread dough benefits from strength and elasticity, while many cakes and pastries need a more tender structure. Flour choice, water, fat, sugar, and mixing all affect the result.

McGee’s discussion of gluten strength shows why changing one ingredient can change several properties. Substituting flour is more than exchanging equal amounts of powder. A useful inference is to identify an ingredient’s structural job before replacing it.

Plant Texture Depends on the Cooking Liquid

Vegetables contain cells whose walls and connecting materials help give them firmness. Cooking changes those structures. The liquid around them influences how quickly they soften.

McGee explains that acid can slow softening. Vegetables cooked in a tomato-based liquid may remain firm much longer than the same vegetables cooked in neutral water. Calcium in hard water can also reinforce connections between cell-wall materials. Alkaline conditions can promote rapid softening and eventually mushiness. These effects appear in his section on acidity, water, and firmness.

This gives the cook another question to ask when a vegetable stays stubbornly firm. The cooking liquid may matter as much as elapsed time. Adding an acidic ingredient later can be useful when tenderness is the goal, though the best sequence depends on the dish.

Sauces and Foams Depend on Arrangement

A sauce’s texture depends on how its parts are distributed. In an emulsion, tiny droplets of one liquid are dispersed in another. For an oil-in-water sauce, emulsifiers coat the oil droplets and help prevent them from joining into a separate pool. Egg yolk supplies such substances. Whisking creates droplets; stabilizing them is another job. McGee explains this division in “Emulsifiers”.

A foam distributes gas bubbles through a liquid instead. Egg-white proteins can help support bubble walls. Yet more beating is not always better. As proteins bond too tightly, the foam can become grainy and release liquid. McGee’s account of foam collapse makes the connection between useful structure and excessive structure clear.

The practical implication is to diagnose the arrangement that failed. A separated sauce and a collapsed foam may both look watery, but they need different explanations. Adding more of the same action can make matters worse.

Microbes Are Also Food Makers

Cooking knowledge extends beyond the stove. McGee places fermentation within the same study of controlled transformation. Yeasts generate gas that helps raise bread. Fermented milk becomes yogurt, a food with a long history across many regions and many uses beyond a sweetened snack.

His sections on yeasts and yogurt connect biological activity with established culinary practices. People developed useful methods long before they understood the organisms involved. Scientific explanation can clarify what a tradition accomplishes without erasing the skill that created it.

Fermentation also illustrates the importance of conditions. McGee describes how unsuitable salt levels, temperatures, or exposure to air can favor unwanted microbes in vegetable fermentations. Understanding a process means understanding what can disrupt it.

How to Use the Book—and Its Limits

A practical way to read McGee is to begin with a specific question. Why did the custard release water? Why did the vegetables stay firm? Read about the relevant ingredient, identify the likely mechanism, and change one controllable factor next time. This is an application of his approach, rather than a prescribed program from the book.

The reference format has limits. It does not supply the complete quantities, timing, and sensory guidance needed for every dish. Explanations must still meet the variation of real ingredients and equipment. Knowing why a sauce separates does not immediately give someone the hand skills to make it well.

The revised edition also reflects knowledge assembled for 2004. Its historical health discussions should not be treated as current dietary guidance. This summary focuses on cooking mechanisms and makes no new health claims.

McGee’s lasting contribution is a way to connect observation with explanation. The cook learns to ask what the food is made of, which change is wanted, and which conditions will encourage it. That knowledge makes recipes easier to understand and experiments easier to interpret.
