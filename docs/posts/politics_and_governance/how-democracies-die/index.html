How Democracies Die by Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt

  • #Politics and Governance

Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt · 8 min read

How elected leaders weaken democracy from within, why parties and unwritten rules matter, and what it takes to defend fair political competition.

How elected leaders can dismantle democracy

A government can hold elections while making it harder for the opposition to win. Courts can remain open while protecting those in power. Newspapers can still appear while their owners face pressure to stay silent. Democratic institutions can survive in name while losing their purpose.

This is the central warning of How Democracies Die. Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt argue that elected leaders can gradually destroy the system that brought them to office. Political parties must resist such leaders, and constitutional rules need support from shared standards of conduct.

Published in 2018, the book draws on European and Latin American history to examine the United States during Donald Trump’s rise and first year as president. This summary presents that argument in its original setting. It does not treat later events as evidence the authors already possessed. Harvard’s publication record confirms the book’s authors, date, and comparative scope.

Why decline can be hard to recognize

The authors contrast an obvious military takeover with a gradual loss of democratic competition. In the second pattern, each measure can look temporary, technical, or lawful. Officials may describe attacks on independent institutions as efforts to improve efficiency or fight corruption.

Venezuela illustrates the danger. Hugo Chávez won the presidency in 1998 by challenging a discredited political establishment. Over time, his government delayed a recall referendum, blacklisted people who signed the recall petition, expanded control over the supreme court, and restricted independent media. Elections continued, but the conditions for competition became increasingly unequal. The publisher’s excerpt traces this progression.

The distinction matters because democracy requires more than counting votes. Citizens need meaningful choices, basic freedoms, and a fair chance to replace their leaders. A single election victory gives a government authority to govern. It does not give it permission to remove those conditions.

Four warning signs of authoritarian behavior

Drawing on political scientist Juan Linz, the authors identify four warning signs. Their test concerns observable conduct rather than a candidate’s ideology or personal charm.

Warning signWhat readers should examine
Weak commitment to democratic rulesDoes the leader reject constitutional limits or refuse to accept credible election results?
Rejection of opponents’ legitimacyDoes the leader portray lawful rivals as enemies who have no right to govern?
Acceptance of political violenceDoes the leader encourage attacks or excuse violence by supporters?
Willingness to restrict civil libertiesDoes the leader threaten the freedoms of critics, opponents, or the press?

The authors say any one of these signs warrants concern. In their assessment, Trump’s 2016 campaign displayed all four. That is their explicit judgment, rather than a neutral classification accepted by everyone. Their published interview explains the test and its application.

The test is a warning system, not proof that dictatorship must follow. A useful application is to ask the same questions about one’s preferred candidate. Otherwise, the checklist can become another way to condemn political opponents while overlooking similar conduct among allies.

Political parties control an important entrance

Dangerous leaders rarely gain power entirely on their own. Established politicians can lend them access, organization, and respectability. They may expect to control the newcomer or use the newcomer’s popularity against a shared opponent.

The book examines this mistake through the rise of Hitler and Mussolini. The authors focus on decisions by established leaders who helped them reach office. The lesson concerns the failure of political judgment; it does not require treating every later demagogue as identical to either dictator.

Parties serve as gatekeepers when they refuse nominations, endorsements, and alliances to figures who reject democratic competition. The difficult test comes when exclusion carries a political cost. Protecting democracy may require supporting a rival who accepts its rules. The publisher’s teaching guide highlights these choices.

This creates a real tension. Party leaders can also protect insiders and exclude legitimate challengers. A democratic party therefore needs a defensible boundary: challenging a policy or establishment is different from rejecting opponents’ right to compete.

How incumbents make competition unfair

Once in office, an aspiring autocrat can weaken checks on power through three connected methods. Ziblatt describes them in his interview with California magazine.

Control the referees. Courts, prosecutors, election authorities, and other public bodies should apply rules independently. Loyal appointments and selective enforcement can turn them into tools against rivals and protection for the government.

Weaken the opposition. Independent journalists, civic groups, business leaders, and opposition politicians can face pressure. The aim may be silence or dependence rather than an outright ban.

Change the rules. Electoral laws and other arrangements can be altered to favor the incumbent. Competition remains possible, but the governing party makes its own defeat less likely.

These methods reinforce one another. A weakened court offers less protection to a targeted newspaper. A quieter press makes changes to electoral rules harder to scrutinize.

Consider a hypothetical local election. A mayor replaces independent election officials with loyalists, threatens a critical newspaper’s access to public advertising, and changes registration procedures to burden opposition neighborhoods. Each act requires separate investigation. Together, they raise a larger question about fair competition. This is an illustration of the framework, not a case reported in the book.

Two unwritten rules help institutions work

Levitsky and Ziblatt emphasize two norms that support constitutional government. They call these democracy’s informal guardrails.

Mutual toleration means accepting lawful opponents as legitimate participants. Parties can disagree strongly while recognizing each other’s right to compete and govern.

Institutional forbearance means using legal powers with restraint. Officials avoid exploiting every available procedure to disable their rivals or make ordinary government impossible.

The norms support each other. If losing means handing power to a legitimate rival, restraint is easier. If losing feels like national destruction, extreme tactics become easier to justify. Opponents then respond, and the conflict escalates. The authorized excerpt in Pomona College Magazine explains this relationship.

Forbearance still leaves room for serious oversight. Investigating misconduct or imposing a justified sanction can protect democracy. The practical judgment concerns purpose, evidence, and consequences. Restraint cannot mean giving officials immunity from accountability.

America’s stability had an exclusionary foundation

The authors complicate any nostalgic appeal to a more civil political past. After Reconstruction, Southern states stripped Black citizens of voting rights. National political cooperation coexisted with entrenched racial exclusion. The publisher’s teaching guide identifies this as a central theme of the book.

Civil rights gains later changed the political system. As voters increasingly sorted into parties by race, religion, and culture, policy disagreements became tied to identity and status. In the authors’ account, fears of losing social dominance helped drive Republican radicalization. They place substantial responsibility on Republican leaders while also describing cycles of retaliation. Their 2018 Harvard interview develops this explanation.

Their goal is inclusive democracy with durable restraint. Restoring calm by sacrificing equal participation would defeat that goal. This is one of the book’s strongest qualifications: political stability and democratic equality are distinct achievements, and both need protection.

Defending democracy requires alliances and choices

The authors recommend broad coalitions that cross ordinary political divisions. Business, religious, civic, and party leaders can disagree on policy while defending fair elections and independent institutions. They also connect lasting democratic repair with reducing economic inequality and building a more inclusive politics.

Their Harvard interview urges active opposition through elections, courts, and legislatures while those channels remain available. It also acknowledges that responding to repeated norm violations involves difficult choices. Automatic retaliation can deepen the damage, while continued restraint can leave abuses unchecked.

A practical reading of this argument is to judge reforms by what they permit future opponents to do. Would a proposed power still seem acceptable if a rival used it? Would an election rule still seem fair after one’s own party lost? These questions do not settle every dispute. They help distinguish durable democratic safeguards from temporary partisan advantages.

What the framework can and cannot explain

The book’s strength is its attention to decisions that occur before a visible collapse. It directs readers toward party alliances, official appointments, enforcement, and political language. These choices are easier to examine than a vague claim that a country has become authoritarian.

Its comparisons require care. Different countries have different courts, party systems, histories, and sources of power. Similar rhetoric does not prove an identical outcome. Nor does the checklist establish a numerical probability of democratic failure.

My main reservation concerns the boundary between restraint and necessary reform. An old practice can protect fair competition, preserve exclusion, or do both. Readers must ask who benefits from a norm and whose rights it protects. The authors’ own discussion of racial exclusion makes that question unavoidable.

The framework is most useful for examining specific conduct. A present-day assessment needs additional evidence about actual enforcement, electoral fairness, civil liberties, and the ability to remove those in office. The book supplies questions for that work; its 2018 diagnosis cannot substitute for it.
