The Life of Charlotte Brontë by Elizabeth Gaskell

  • #Biographies

Elizabeth Gaskell · 6 min read

First biography of the Jane Eyre author, written by her friend and fellow novelist

Elizabeth Gaskell’s “The Life of Charlotte Brontë,” published in 1857, is not merely a biography; it’s a poignant portrait of a remarkable woman grappling with adversity and achieving literary immortality against daunting odds. Gaskell, a friend and fellow writer, crafted a sensitive and insightful narrative that reveals the complexities of Charlotte Brontë’s life, highlighting her personal struggles, creative genius, and enduring legacy. The book remains a cornerstone of Brontë scholarship, offering a deeply personal and historically significant account of the author’s life.

A Childhood Forged in Hardship and Imagination

The narrative begins with a bleak yet deeply evocative portrayal of Charlotte’s childhood. Born into the impoverished Irish parsonage of Haworth, Yorkshire, Charlotte and her siblings faced early loss and hardship. The premature deaths of her mother and several sisters instilled in her a profound sense of loss and a deep understanding of mortality, themes that would consistently permeate her work. Gaskell vividly depicts the isolation of their life, the stark landscape of the Yorkshire moors shaping their imaginations, and the fiercely independent spirit fostered within the close-knit family unit. Their father, Patrick Brontë, a stern but ultimately loving figure, provided a crucial intellectual foundation, encouraging their reading and writing pursuits despite limited educational opportunities. The children’s imaginative games, including the creation of the fantastical Gondal and Angria, are presented as formative experiences that nurtured their creative abilities and laid the groundwork for their future literary endeavors. This early life, filled with both profound sorrow and extraordinary imagination, created the fertile ground from which Charlotte’s literary talent would bloom.

Education and the Seeds of Rebellion

The Brontës’ limited formal education highlights the societal constraints faced by women in 19th-century England. Gaskell details Charlotte’s brief and unhappy time at Cowan Bridge School, a harsh institution that mirrored the bleak settings of her later novels. This experience, marked by illness and deprivation, left a lasting impact on Charlotte, informing the stark depictions of institutional cruelty found in Jane Eyre. Later, the family’s efforts to secure a better education for the sisters highlight their determination and resourcefulness. The Brontës’ self-education, fueled by their voracious reading and intellectual curiosity, is presented as a testament to their resilience and unwavering thirst for knowledge. Their unconventional education, while lacking in traditional structure, fostered their independent thinking and allowed them to develop their unique literary voices, unburdened by societal expectations.

The Path to Publication and Literary Success

Gaskell carefully traces Charlotte’s journey from aspiring writer to acclaimed novelist. The challenges faced by women seeking literary recognition in the Victorian era are vividly illustrated through the pseudonyms used by the Brontë sisters – Currer, Ellis, and Acton Bell – concealing their gender to navigate the male-dominated literary world. The initial rejection of their manuscripts serves as a testament to their perseverance and determination. The eventual publication of Jane Eyre in 1847 marked a turning point, catapulting Charlotte into literary prominence. Gaskell skillfully captures the excitement and apprehension surrounding the novel’s success, acknowledging the both the initial controversies sparked by its radical themes and the subsequent critical acclaim that solidified Charlotte’s place in the literary landscape. The success of Jane Eyre, followed by Shirley and Villette, not only established her reputation but also challenged the prevailing social norms through her portrayal of strong, independent female characters.

Personal Relationships and the Weight of Loss

Gaskell’s biography delves deeply into Charlotte’s personal relationships, underscoring the emotional complexities of her life. Her passionate but ultimately tragic relationship with Constantin Heger, a teacher during her time in Brussels, is presented with sensitivity and nuance, acknowledging the profound influence he had on her personal and professional development while acknowledging the unrequited nature of her affections. The narrative underscores the significance of this relationship in shaping her artistic sensibilities, highlighting the emotional intensity that informs her portrayals of love and loss in her novels. The deaths of her beloved sisters Emily and Anne are depicted with poignant grief, emphasizing the devastating impact of these losses on Charlotte’s emotional well-being. These experiences, while deeply painful, are presented as formative influences on her literary work, underscoring the intricate connection between personal loss and artistic expression.

Marriage and a Tragic End

The final chapter of Charlotte’s life, marked by her marriage to Arthur Bell Nicholls, her father’s curate, provides a counterpoint to the earlier periods of loneliness and hardship. Gaskell portrays this relationship as a source of genuine happiness and companionship, offering a glimpse into a side of Charlotte less frequently explored. However, this happiness was short-lived. Charlotte’s pregnancy and subsequent death from complications emphasize the harsh realities faced by women in the 19th century, highlighting the fragility of life and the tragic irony of her achieving literary success only to be cut down at the peak of her creative powers.

Literary Legacy and Lasting Influence

Gaskell’s biography transcends a mere chronological account; it offers a compelling exploration of Charlotte Brontë’s literary legacy. The enduring appeal of Brontë’s novels, particularly Jane Eyre, is attributed to their exploration of universal themes: love, loss, social injustice, and the complexities of the human spirit. Gaskell highlights the powerful female characters that populate Brontë’s works, showcasing the author’s ability to create relatable and compelling heroines who defy conventional societal expectations. The realism woven into the romantic narratives, the exploration of class divisions, and the frank portrayal of emotional depth cemented Brontë’s influence on subsequent generations of writers. Gaskell’s biography emphasizes the enduring power of Brontë’s novels to resonate with readers across time and cultures, solidifying her place as a pivotal figure in English literature.

Controversies and Interpretations

Gaskell’s biography, while lauded for its sensitivity and insight, has not been without its criticisms. Certain aspects of the narrative, especially the portrayal of Charlotte’s relationship with Constantin Heger, have been subject to differing interpretations. Modern scholars have revisited and reinterpreted some aspects of the biography, offering alternative perspectives and engaging in a deeper exploration of the complexities of Charlotte Brontë’s life and relationships. However, Gaskell’s work remains a vital primary source, offering an invaluable window into the life and mind of one of English literature’s most iconic figures.

In conclusion, Elizabeth Gaskell’s “The Life of Charlotte Brontë” is a powerful and enduring testament to the life and accomplishments of a remarkable woman. It’s a biography that not only chronicles the significant events of Charlotte’s life but also delves into her inner world, capturing her struggles, her triumphs, and the enduring legacy of her literary genius. Gaskell’s compassionate and insightful portrayal establishes the enduring fascination with Charlotte Brontë, ensuring that her story continues to inspire and move readers for generations to come. The book serves as a powerful reminder of the extraordinary strength, resilience, and talent of a woman who defied societal constraints and left an indelible mark on the world of literature.
