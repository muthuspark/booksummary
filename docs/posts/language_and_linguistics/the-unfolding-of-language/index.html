The Unfolding of Language by Guy Deutscher

  • #Language and Linguistics

Guy Deutscher · 8 min read

How erosion, metaphor, and analogy turn everyday speech into complex grammar, and what these changes suggest about early language.

How everyday speech builds complex grammar

Languages have complex rules. These rules let speakers combine words, refer to different times, and show who does what. They also let people understand sentences they have never heard before. Yet no one planned the whole system.

In The Unfolding of Language, Guy Deutscher asks how everyday communication can create this structure. His answer connects two kinds of change. Some changes reduce sounds or weaken meanings. Others create new patterns and grammatical functions. The same expression can undergo both kinds of change.

Speakers extend familiar expressions to new uses. Repeated use can shorten these expressions. Speakers and learners then organize them into patterns. Over generations, these changes can hide the origins of a complex grammatical system. This link between loss and creation is the book’s main idea. Macmillan’s book description

Deutscher mainly examines how language structure develops through history and shared use. He does not resolve how humans first acquired the biological and mental abilities needed for language.

Three pressures that change language

Deutscher identifies three main pressures: economy, expressiveness, and analogy.

  • Economy leads speakers to reduce effort. For example, they may shorten a common expression.
  • Expressiveness leads speakers to make a message stronger, clearer, or more distinctive.
  • Analogy leads speakers and learners to extend a familiar pattern to other forms.

These pressures can work against each other. A speaker may use a longer expression to add emphasis. With repeated use, the expression becomes familiar and predictable. Speakers may then shorten it. Meanwhile, learners find patterns in what they hear. They sometimes apply those patterns to forms that previously followed a different rule.

Deutscher describes these interacting pressures as cycles of expansion and reduction. Author interview, Children of the Code

A speaker’s immediate purpose differs from the long-term result. A person may want to save effort or make a point more clearly. If a community adopts the same usage, it can become a grammatical rule. No one needs to plan that result.

Erosion can hide earlier forms

Sounds can change or disappear over time. These changes can hide an earlier connection between words and grammatical forms.

Meanings can also become less specific. An expression with a vivid meaning may become a routine way to express a grammatical relation. Linguists call this loss of specific word meaning semantic bleaching.

Semantic bleaching can form part of grammaticalization. In this process, a word or expression gains a grammatical function or develops a more grammatical use. For example, a separate word can become an ending that marks tense.

Bernd Heine and Tania Kuteva describe several changes involved in grammaticalization. An expression can change its meaning, appear in new contexts, change its grammatical behavior, or lose sounds. As it loses some independence or concrete meaning, it can gain a new grammatical function. World Lexicon of Grammaticalization, publisher excerpt

A short ending may therefore preserve part of a longer expression. Its current form alone may give little clue to its history. An apparently arbitrary form can make more sense when we know how it developed.

Metaphor connects concrete and abstract ideas

Deutscher treats metaphor as a common part of language. People use familiar physical experiences to describe abstract ideas. They do this in ordinary speech as well as in literature. Authorized excerpts from the book

Consider these English expressions:

  • To grasp an argument is to understand it. The expression connects understanding with holding an object.
  • To follow an explanation is to understand its sequence. The expression connects understanding with movement along a route.
  • A distant possibility seems unlikely or remote. The expression uses physical distance to describe an abstract judgment.

Speakers usually understand these expressions without consciously forming a metaphor.

Similar changes can help create grammar. A word for a body part can develop a use that describes position. An expression for movement can develop a use that refers to the future. Heine and Kuteva discuss both types of change. The context in which people use an expression helps shape its new function. World Lexicon of Grammaticalization, publisher excerpt

A grammatical word may carry little concrete meaning. It can still do essential work by showing how parts of a sentence relate to each other.

How a phrase gains a grammatical function

English going to provides a clear example. Compare these sentences:

  • “I am going to the station.”
  • “I am going to revise the report.”
  • “The roof is going to collapse.”

The first sentence describes movement toward a place. The second expresses an intention. It does not require the speaker to travel anywhere. The third predicts an event. It does not suggest that the roof has an intention.

Cambridge’s grammar reference distinguishes movement from future uses of going to. These future uses include plans, predictions, and commands. Cambridge, “Going to”

The three sentences illustrate different uses in present-day English. They do not establish the exact historical steps between those uses. They show how an expression can work beyond its physical meaning.

The French future tense provides a historical example. Nous chanterons means “we will sing.” It developed from a construction that combined an infinitive with a form of Latin habere, meaning “have.” An infinitive is a verb form such as English “to sing.” Material that once stood separately became a verb ending.

Hopper and Traugott discuss this change alongside the newer French construction nous allons chanter. This construction literally means “we are going to sing.” Grammaticalization, publisher excerpt

A verb ending does not need to begin as a specially designed part of grammar. It can develop from words that once had more independent uses.

Analogy builds patterns

Analogy extends a familiar pattern to another case. A child who says goed applies the regular English past-tense ending to go. Standard English uses went, but the child’s form follows a clear rule.

Deutscher emphasizes the role of learners in organizing language. They find patterns in the forms they hear. The system they develop may differ slightly from the system used by the previous generation. Author interview

The book applies this argument to the Semitic verb system. Related words share consonant roots. Other changes help express differences in grammar and word meaning. Deutscher examines how sound change and analogy could build these complex patterns through small steps. Kenny Smith’s scholarly review identifies this discussion as a major example of the book’s argument. LINGUIST List review

Analogy can make forms more regular without making the whole system simple. Extending a pattern may produce a highly organized but complex system. Regularity and ease of learning are related, but they are different measures.

Language change does not necessarily mean decline

Deutscher traces complaints about declining English through several generations. People criticized the language even during periods that later writers treated as golden ages. These complaints challenge the idea that unfamiliar usage proves a general loss of quality. Book excerpts

We can separate judgments about a particular use from claims about the whole language. An editor may choose a standard form because readers will understand it more easily. That choice does not prove that another form reduces the language’s overall ability to express ideas.

History can also help learners understand irregular forms. However, understanding why a form developed does not remove the need to learn its current use.

These are applications of the book’s argument, rather than writing rules that Deutscher proposes. They encourage us to examine how people communicate before treating a personal preference as evidence of decline.

What the explanation leaves open

In the final reconstruction, Deutscher asks a broader question. Could the processes seen in historical languages build complex grammar from a much simpler starting point?

Smith describes this as a uniformitarian approach. It uses known processes to limit possible explanations of a past that we cannot observe directly. The result is an account of plausible mechanisms. It does not recover the actual speech of prehistoric people. LINGUIST List review

Three limits matter:

  • A starting point with meaningful words already assumes much. It leaves the origin of those words and the abilities needed to use them unexplained.
  • A possible route does not prove that every language followed it.
  • Cycles of change do not follow a fixed schedule. Heine and Kuteva note that grammaticalization can stop. Forms that lose sounds or specific meaning are not always replaced.

Deutscher’s account helps explain how everyday speaking and learning can produce complex grammar. We should distinguish those observable mechanisms from reconstructions of early language. A plausible explanation of how grammar could develop still needs further evidence to establish what actually happened.
