The Language Instinct by Steven Pinker

  • #Language and Linguistics

Steven Pinker · 8 min read

Pinker explains language as an evolved human capacity, from children's grammar and speech perception to the debate over innate rules.

How the human mind makes language possible

A child can understand a sentence that no one has taught them. An adult can describe an event that has never happened. Both abilities depend on more than a store of remembered phrases.

In The Language Instinct, first published in 1994, Steven Pinker argues that humans inherit specialized mental capacities for language. Children develop language through these capacities as they interact with a language community. Schools did not invent the underlying ability. Pinker presents language as an evolved part of human nature. His book description places this argument within a wider account of speech, learning, thought, and evolution.

The book changes the question we ask about ordinary conversation. Instead of asking only whether someone speaks correctly, we can ask what mental work makes their speech possible.

An instinct for learning, rather than a language at birth

Pinker uses instinct to describe a complex capacity that develops without formal lessons. He does not mean that babies arrive knowing English, Japanese, or any other particular language. The inherited capacity must work with experience.

He draws on Noam Chomsky’s proposal that learners bring an internal framework to the task. Universal grammar, in this account, constrains the kinds of grammatical systems children can acquire. Children still have to learn the words and patterns of their community.

Pinker also points to language across societies and to differences between language ability and other mental abilities. Brain injuries and developmental conditions can affect language unevenly. He interprets this evidence as support for specialized mental organization. His first-person account of the argument makes these connections explicit.

The distinction matters. Saying that learning needs biological machinery does not tell us exactly how much grammar that machinery contains. That stronger question runs through the book and its critics.

Grammar gives a small vocabulary enormous reach

The book explains how words combine into structured expressions. Syntax concerns these combinations. A sentence has relationships between its parts, rather than merely a sequence of neighboring words. The publisher-supplied overview and contents identify this explanation as a central part of Pinker’s project.

Consider two examples constructed for this summary:

  • “The child follows the dog.”
  • “The dog follows the child.”

The vocabulary stays the same. The event changes because the words occupy different roles. Knowing the meanings of child, dog, and follow is insufficient. A listener must also recover who acts and who receives the action.

Now consider “the child beside the windows is singing.” The plural word windows appears close to is. Yet the child determines the verb’s singular form. This illustrates why grammar needs relationships that can extend across intervening words.

Such structure helps explain linguistic creativity. We can add a description to a noun, put a clause inside another clause, or combine familiar parts in a new way. We do not need a separate memorized entry for every possible sentence.

Children’s errors reveal productive knowledge

Pinker distinguishes stored words from operations that combine them. A regular form such as walked follows a productive pattern. An irregular form such as went requires knowledge of a particular word. His research article “Words and rules” develops this distinction and considers competing explanations.

A child who says goed produces a useful error. The form is wrong in standard English, but it follows a pattern. The child has extended a past-tense operation to a verb that requires an exception. Simple imitation cannot explain an utterance that differs from the adult model in this systematic way.

This does not mean that each error proves an innate grammar rule. A learner could discover a pattern through experience. The narrower lesson is that language learning involves generalization. Children build a system that lets them go beyond the examples they have heard.

For a parent, that changes the meaning of a mistake. An error can reveal progress in finding a pattern. It does not necessarily show that the child has forgotten everything they previously knew.

Hearing speech means solving a hidden problem

The book also examines speech production and perception. Spoken language reaches the listener as a changing physical signal. It does not arrive with the neat visual spaces found on a printed page.

To see the problem, imagine hearing an unfamiliar language. You may hear a continuous stream without knowing where one word ends. Familiar speech feels more clearly divided because the listener already knows much of the system. This contrast helps expose work that ordinary listening hides.

Later research supplies an important complement to Pinker’s account. Jenny Saffran, Richard Aslin, and Elissa Newport showed that infants could use statistical relationships between neighboring sounds to find candidate words in an artificial speech stream. Their 1996 experiment demonstrated a learning mechanism that does not require an adult to explain word boundaries.

The result concerns one part of language acquisition. It does not explain the whole of grammar. It does show why spontaneous learning should not be confused with learning that uses little information. A child can extract structure from exposure without receiving explicit instruction.

Thought is broader than the words used to express it

Pinker rejects the strong claim that a person’s language determines what they can think. He proposes mentalese: an internal system for representing thoughts that differs from any spoken language. The book’s discussion of thought asks us to separate a message from the words chosen to express it.

An everyday illustration is the effort to revise an unclear sentence. A writer may know that the words fail to express the intended idea. That experience suggests a distinction between an intention and its verbal form. It does not, by itself, establish Pinker’s full theory of mental representation.

Later evidence also calls for care about weaker claims. Jonathan Winawer and colleagues found that Russian speakers’ color categories affected performance in a blue-color discrimination task. Verbal interference removed the relevant advantage. Their 2007 study supports an influence of language on a particular task.

That finding does not establish that vocabulary sets the limits of thought. It does make “language influences thinking” a more defensible claim than “language has no effect on thinking.” The strength and context of the claim matter.

Why Pinker brings evolution into the explanation

An inherited capacity requires an account of its origins. Pinker argues that natural selection shaped language for communication. He differs here from accounts that treat language mainly as a by-product of other evolutionary changes.

The argument rests on a fit between mechanism and function. Language coordinates many operations to transmit structured ideas. Communication can help people share knowledge, coordinate activity, and manage social relationships. Pinker and Paul Bloom set out this case in “Natural language and natural selection”.

Two histories must remain separate. One concerns the evolution of human capacities across generations. The other concerns a child learning a particular language. A child learning English does not replay the evolutionary origin of language.

An adaptation argument also leaves historical questions open. Explaining why a capacity could improve communication does not establish the precise sequence by which it evolved. The book offers an explanatory framework, rather than a direct record of prehistoric speech.

Grammar and social standards answer different questions

Pinker separates the unconscious grammar of conversation from conventions enforced in formal writing. A speaker can use a dialect with systematic rules while departing from a school’s preferred forms. He argues against treating those departures as evidence of defective language ability. His discussion of vernacular and prescriptive grammar explains the distinction.

A practical implication is to state the purpose of a correction. An editor may need a standard form for a shared audience. A teacher may need to explain a written convention. Those needs do not require a judgment that the speaker lacks grammar.

Readers can make the same distinction when assessing an unfamiliar accent or expression. Ask whether the difficulty comes from unclear meaning, an unfamiliar convention, or a social expectation. These are different problems and may need different responses.

What remains disputed

The book’s clearest contribution is to make ordinary language look like a scientific problem. Its stronger theory of specialized, inherited grammar remains contested.

Nicholas Evans and Stephen C. Levinson argue that languages show much greater structural diversity than accounts of a common grammatical design often acknowledge. Their 2009 research review emphasizes cultural history, learning, and constraints from human cognition. This challenges inferences from familiar languages to a detailed universal blueprint. It does not imply that language operates without biological support.

The most useful reading therefore separates three questions: What can humans do with language? How do children acquire that ability? What inherited organization makes acquisition possible? Evidence for one answer does not automatically settle the others.

Pinker gives readers a powerful starting point: fluent speech depends on intricate mental activity. The continuing task is to explain how inherited capacities, experience, and social use build that ability together.
