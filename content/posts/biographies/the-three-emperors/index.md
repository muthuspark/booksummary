## Three cousins before the First World War

Miranda Carter's *The Three Emperors: Three Cousins, Three Empires and the Road to World War One* follows George V of Britain, Wilhelm II of Germany, and Nicholas II of Russia.

Carter connects their family relationships with the diplomatic world in which they ruled. Kinship did not guarantee cooperation. Rivalries, insecurities, and conflicting national interests repeatedly undermined the idea that Europe's ruling families could keep international tensions under control.

## Private character and public power

The book uses the cousins' personalities to make the workings of monarchy more tangible. Their habits and personal quarrels matter because they occupied positions with international consequences. Family familiarity could encourage misunderstanding as readily as trust.

The wider story follows Europe's deteriorating relations before the First World War. A useful distinction is between the family drama and the political systems surrounding it: personal relationships were part of the crisis, rather than a sufficient explanation for everything that followed. Carter's joint biography brings those private and public histories into the same frame.
