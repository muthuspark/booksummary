# How Government Built Residential Segregation

Why do American neighborhoods remain divided by race? A familiar answer points to personal prejudice, differences in income, and private decisions about where to live. Richard Rothstein's *The Color of Law: A Forgotten History of How Our Government Segregated America* challenges the adequacy of that explanation.

First published in 2017, the book assembles historical cases from across the United States. **Its central claim is that federal, state, and local governments deliberately created and reinforced residential segregation.** Public officials did more than tolerate discrimination. They organized housing opportunities around racial exclusion. The [Economic Policy Institute's book overview](https://www.epi.org/publication/the-color-of-law-a-forgotten-history-of-how-our-government-segregated-america/) establishes this argument and the book's original publication.

For Rothstein, recognizing that history changes the question of responsibility. If segregation arose through unconstitutional public action, government has an obligation to help repair it. Ending an openly discriminatory rule does not necessarily end the conditions that rule produced.

## The Difference Between Private Choice and Public Action

Rothstein builds his argument around two terms. **De facto segregation** means separation that exists in practice, commonly attributed to private conduct or social conditions. **De jure segregation** means separation imposed through law and official policy.

The distinction affects what remedies seem justified. If people simply chose separate neighborhoods, integration can appear to require interference with private preferences. If public authorities constrained those choices, continued separation becomes a problem of uncorrected public harm.

Rothstein does not need to show that private racism was absent. His argument concerns the public machinery that made exclusion durable. As the [publisher's account](https://wwnorton.co.uk/books/9781631494536-the-color-of-law) explains, that machinery included housing programs, subsidies, zoning, tax benefits, and official support for resistance to Black neighbors.

The useful question therefore becomes specific: which institution helped create a particular boundary, and how? A racial pattern alone does not explain its own history.

## Public Housing Could Create the Separation It Claimed to Reflect

Public housing is often imagined as a response to existing poverty and segregation. Rothstein shows that some projects actively reshaped mixed neighborhoods into racially separate communities.

His account of Atlanta's Techwood Homes is especially clear. The Public Works Administration cleared a neighborhood that included both Black and white households. The replacement project admitted white families only. Displaced Black families could not simply return to new homes on the same site. Their restricted alternatives increased pressure on other Black neighborhoods.

The agency's supposed respect for a neighborhood's racial composition could also conceal a circular process. Officials designated an area for one race, built housing on that basis, and helped make the designation a reality. In other places, separate projects hardened divisions within previously mixed communities. These examples appear in [authorized excerpts from the book](https://www.learningforjustice.org/sites/default/files/2019-10/COL%20Lesson%201%20Housing%20Book%20Excerpts%20Handout_vF%20PDF.pdf).

This reverses a common explanation of overcrowding. A crowded neighborhood need not show that residents preferred to live apart. It can show that authorities restricted where they could live while displacing them from other areas.

## Mortgage Support Built Unequal Routes to Ownership

A second mechanism operated through private construction supported by government. Federal mortgage insurance and loan guarantees helped make suburban development possible. Rothstein emphasizes that public support often came with racial conditions.

The Federal Housing Administration's underwriting standards treated Black occupancy near white homes as a threat to property values. The Veterans Administration followed the same general approach. Government therefore helped expand ownership for white working families while excluding Black families from many of the developments it supported.

Levittown illustrates the larger system. Its importance lies in the connection between an apparently private housing market and public backing. A developer's exclusionary sales policy did not operate in isolation from government finance.

In [later Senate testimony restating the book's argument](https://www.epi.org/blog/powerful-government-policy-segregated-us-the-same-can-desegregate-us-says-color-of-law-author-richard-rothstein/), Rothstein explains the lasting effect: owners gained equity as homes rose in value. That wealth could support education, absorb emergencies, and help the next generation buy homes.

The denied opportunity was therefore larger than a house purchase. It included access to an asset whose benefits could accumulate and pass between generations. Income measures alone miss this distinction between what a household earns and what it owns.

## Zoning and Courts Helped Sustain the Boundaries

Local land-use decisions supplied another part of the system. Rules about lot sizes and permitted housing types could keep less expensive homes out of desirable suburbs. Their language could concern buildings while their purpose concerned the people allowed to occupy them.

Rothstein's [research on the St. Louis region](https://www.epi.org/publication/making-ferguson/), which provides background to the book's argument, traces how zoning, public housing, redevelopment, and private restrictions worked together. Some planning records explicitly connected land-use decisions to racial separation.

Restrictive covenants added a legal barrier. These were agreements barring the sale or occupancy of property by specified racial groups. Their force depended partly on courts willing to enforce them. In *Shelley v. Kraemer* in 1948, the Supreme Court held that judicial enforcement of racial covenants violated the Fourteenth Amendment.

The distinction matters: private agreement and public enforcement were connected. Describing the covenant as private can hide the state's role in making it effective. Removing one legal tool also left other barriers available. Housing finance, zoning, and decisions about where to build still shaped the choices families could make.

## Market Outcomes Could Reflect Earlier Exclusion

Rothstein also challenges the assumption that racial turnover simply reveals free preferences. An [authorized excerpt about East Palo Alto](https://www.altaonline.com/california-book-club/a74159240/richard-rothstein-color-of-law-book-excerpt/) describes how exclusion elsewhere and exploitative real-estate practices helped transform the community.

Families denied access to a broad housing market had fewer alternatives. High purchase costs could force households to take in additional occupants to meet payments. Crowding and financial pressure then appeared to confirm the prejudice used to justify exclusion in the first place.

This is an important causal lesson. A condition observed at the end of a process cannot automatically explain what started it. If policy restricts supply for a group, the resulting prices and living conditions are not independent evidence that the group caused neighborhood decline.

This does not make every price difference proof of discrimination. It means that prices need to be understood alongside the rules and practices that shaped access to the market.

## Residential Boundaries Also Shape Schools and Opportunity

A home provides access to more than shelter. Its location affects schools, transport, employment, and the people and institutions a family can reach.

Rothstein's contribution to a [National Academies workshop on housing and education](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK565026/) explains why residential segregation matters to school segregation. When school attendance follows neighborhood boundaries, separating homes also separates children. A school system cannot be fully understood by examining classroom policy alone.

This gives the book implications beyond housing departments. Education policy can struggle against disadvantages produced outside school. Employment opportunities can be difficult to use when affordable homes lie far from jobs.

My reading is that Rothstein asks readers to assess public systems together. A housing decision can produce an education problem. A transport decision can deepen an employment barrier. Treating each outcome as an isolated failure can conceal the decisions that connect them.

## Ending Discrimination Does Not Restore a Lost Opportunity

The Fair Housing Act of 1968 was a major break with explicit housing discrimination. Rothstein nevertheless argues that a right to buy later cannot restore the opportunity to buy earlier, before prices increased and owners accumulated equity.

In a [2017 public discussion of the book](https://www.spl.org/Seattle-Public-Library/documents/transcriptions/2017/17-11-16_Richard-Rothstein.pdf), he uses subsidized access to formerly exclusionary suburbs as a provocative example of repair. He also discusses reducing exclusionary zoning and placing assisted housing in areas with greater opportunity. These are proposals and illustrations of his reasoning, not descriptions of a universally available remedy.

His first priority is historical understanding. Public support for substantial repair is unlikely if citizens believe government had little role in creating the harm. Accurate teaching about housing policy therefore belongs within his reform argument.

## What the Book Establishes—and What It Leaves Open

The book's strength is its account of identifiable mechanisms. It replaces a vague story about prejudice with questions about agencies, finance, construction, courts, and enforcement. It also shows why apparently ordinary administrative decisions deserve historical scrutiny.

Its limits require care. Historical cases do not by themselves measure the contribution of every cause to every present-day racial gap. The book centers Black-white segregation and cannot serve as a complete history of all groups' housing experiences. Local histories also differ.

Rothstein's constitutional argument should be read as an argument for public responsibility. It is not a promise that a court will order any particular reform. Choosing remedies requires further work on legal authority, costs, effectiveness, and the priorities of affected residents.

A practical implication is to investigate a community's housing history before treating its current boundaries as natural. Ask who received public assistance, who was excluded, where displaced families could go, and which benefits survived after the original rule ended. That inquiry makes responsibility more concrete—and gives proposals for repair a firmer basis.
