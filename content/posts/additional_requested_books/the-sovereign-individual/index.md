## When wealth can move, power must bargain

*The Sovereign Individual* asks what happens to government when people can earn and move wealth across borders more easily. James Dale Davidson and William Rees-Mogg predict that information technology will shift power toward mobile individuals. Governments will increasingly compete for their business.

First published in 1997, the book combines historical interpretation, political forecasting, and advice for adapting to change. The later subtitle, *Mastering the Transition to the Information Age*, captures its focus. The [publisher's description and excerpt](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/The-Sovereign-Individual/James-Dale-Davidson/9780684832722) establish the central forecast. It is a strong claim about a possible future, not a neutral account of an inevitable transition.

## Megapolitics: what makes power possible?

The authors call their method **megapolitics**. They look beneath elections and policy debates to the conditions that determine who can exercise force profitably. Technology can change the cost of conquest, protection, and control. Institutions that once worked may then lose their advantage.

Their historical comparison is between gunpowder's disruption of the feudal order and computing's expected disruption of the nation-state. The question is who can defend resources, extract income, and resist demands. A [detailed critical review](https://nontechceo.co.uk/book-notes/the-sovereign-individual/) identifies this argument about the returns to violence as the book's organizing principle.

An illustrative comparison makes the mechanism concrete. A ruler can seize a warehouse and control its goods. Seizing a building does not give the same control over knowledge copied elsewhere. Both forms of wealth need protection, but the means of controlling them differ.

This lens encourages a useful habit: ask what makes an institution's authority enforceable. A formal rule matters partly because someone can apply it. Yet the lens also creates a risk. If every historical change becomes evidence for one theory, the theory becomes hard to challenge. Geography, beliefs, cooperation, and political choices need examination in their own right.

## The sovereign individual has a credible option to leave

In the authors' forecast, a sovereign individual can move income-producing activity between jurisdictions. The ability to leave increases bargaining power. Governments must offer attractive protection and services because valuable residents and businesses have alternatives.

The publisher's excerpt describes citizenship shifting toward a customer relationship. Sovereignty becomes something offered competitively rather than an unquestioned territorial monopoly. Smaller jurisdictions and private providers could take on more of its functions.

Consider an imagined software consultant choosing where to work. If clients care about results rather than location, the consultant can compare several places. An owner whose livelihood depends on a particular harbor has fewer options. The difference is the cost of leaving.

This example also exposes an important boundary. A laptop does not remove family ties, language barriers, health needs, or dependence on reliable courts. Mobility is a capacity that varies between people. It should be assessed through actual alternatives rather than assumed from access to a device.

## Digital money is part of the political argument

The book anticipates online payments, banking, and investment that can operate beyond familiar national channels. It expects this activity to make government control over money and taxation much harder. Greg Kaza's [1998 review for the Foundation for Economic Education](https://fee.org/articles/the-sovereign-individual-how-to-survive-and-thrive-during-the-collapse-of-the-welfare-state-by-james-dale-davidson-and-lord-william-rees-mogg/) quotes the authors' forecast of cybercurrency and network-based financial services.

The important connection is between payment and enforcement. A transaction can cross a border without the goods passing through a customs gate. But several distinct questions remain: who identifies the participants, who holds their assets, who settles disputes, and who can interrupt access?

These distinctions matter when evaluating the forecast. Sending information globally does not establish that an activity sits outside every jurisdiction. A business may still need an identifiable customer, an enforceable contract, and a way to turn receipts into everyday purchases. Each dependency can become a point of control.

## Work becomes more flexible, and security more uneven

Davidson and Rees-Mogg expect networks to reduce the need for corporate layers that coordinate and monitor work. Temporary groups could assemble for specific projects. Kaza's contemporary review also describes their warning that workers who lose income and status could support nationalist resistance to the new economy.

The forecast is therefore both hopeful and harsh. Greater freedom for people with portable skills could accompany insecurity for people whose work loses protection or bargaining power.

The [Publishers Weekly review](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780684810072) confirms the book's wider prediction that labor unions, regulated professions, and political intermediaries would lose influence. Its sovereign individuals form an information elite, rather than a guarantee of equal independence for everyone.

For an illustrative example, compare a specialist who can sell expertise to distant clients with a worker whose customers must be nearby. The same network may expand the specialist's market while exposing the local worker to new competition. Their experiences of technological progress can differ without either person misunderstanding the change.

This helps explain why an account of efficiency cannot settle a political dispute. People also care about security, fairness, and their place in a community. Calling resistance irrational can conceal real losses.

## A difficult transition, not a peaceful escape

The book expects instability as established powers lose control. Its forecasts include new forms of sovereignty and a larger role for criminal groups and covert power. The contemporary *Publishers Weekly* review records these darker elements alongside the promise of autonomy.

This creates a tension worth retaining. Competition among protection providers might improve service. It might also leave a person facing a powerful provider with little effective appeal. The ability to choose a contract is only useful if the choice is real and the contract can be enforced.

Ask what happens after a dispute. Can a customer change providers? Can a weaker party obtain a remedy? Who protects someone who cannot pay? These questions test the proposed replacement institutions as carefully as the book tests the state.

## What later evidence changes

The strongest forecast goes beyond digital commerce becoming common. It expects that commerce to undermine the state's capacity to tax and govern. Those are separate propositions.

A concrete counterweight is international tax cooperation. The [OECD's account of its transparency work](https://www.oecd.org/en/networks/global-forum-tax-transparency/what-we-do.html) describes automatic exchange of financial-account information. Its Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework extends this approach to crypto-asset transactions, with participating jurisdictions working toward exchanges beginning in 2027, 2028, or 2029.

This does not prove that governments can control every transaction. It shows a response the forecast must account for: governments can coordinate and use digital systems to strengthen reporting. The same broad technological change can support private mobility and public enforcement.

There is also a political judgment embedded in the book. Treating taxes mainly as charges for individual services leaves unresolved questions about shared infrastructure, social insurance, and obligations to others. The [Kirkus review](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/a/james-dale-william-rees-mogg-davidson-2/the-sovereign-individual-how-to-survive-and-thr/) warned at publication that ideological commitments shape the vision.

The distinction between prediction and preference is essential. Showing that some people can leave does not show that their departure produces a fairer society. Showing that an institution has weaknesses does not establish that its replacement will perform better.

## How to use the argument

The most useful application is to examine dependence. The following questions apply the book's reasoning without assuming its forecasts will come true:

- **What can travel with your work?** Identify skills, relationships, and records that remain useful across employers or locations.
- **Where does control remain concentrated?** Consider dependence on a single client, platform, payment service, or local institution.
- **What would leaving actually cost?** Include family responsibilities, lost trust, and the effort of building a new network.
- **What supports your independence?** Count the courts, infrastructure, and other people's work that make your options possible.

A practical response might be to develop a skill that serves more than one market or keep usable copies of essential business records. These are applications offered here, rather than promises of safety or a program prescribed by the authors.

The book rewards readers who follow its questions about enforceable power while testing its conclusions. Its lasting challenge is to notice when changing technology alters who can demand terms, who can refuse them, and who must absorb the cost.
