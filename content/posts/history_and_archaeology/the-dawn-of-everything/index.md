# Human history as an experiment in how to live together

Did farming make inequality inevitable? Do large cities need powerful rulers? *The Dawn of Everything: A New History of Humanity* challenges both assumptions. Anthropologist David Graeber and archaeologist David Wengrow argue that the human past contains many ways of organizing society. People adopted, rejected, and changed political arrangements rather than following one fixed path toward modern states.

The [publisher's description and excerpt](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780374157357/thedawnofeverything/) establish the book's central aim: replace a simple story of social development with a wider account of human possibilities. Its question is why the freedom to remake society became so difficult to exercise.

## Replace the ladder of progress

The familiar story runs from small hunting bands to farming villages, then to cities and states. One version celebrates civilization as an escape from violence. Another mourns it as the loss of an earlier equality. In both, larger populations and new technologies narrow political choice.

Graeber and Wengrow reject this shared assumption. As their [authorized opening excerpt](https://lithub.com/the-dawn-of-everything-is-not-a-book-about-the-origins-of-inequality/) explains, neither a peaceful beginning nor a brutal beginning adequately describes human diversity. Early people were capable of political thought. They could compare institutions, resist leaders, and imagine alternatives.

This changes the historical task. Instead of finding the moment when humanity lost equality, examine how particular forms of command became permanent. Differences in wealth, prestige, and the power to force obedience require separate explanations.

## Authority could change with the season

One of the book's strongest ideas is that a society need not have one political structure throughout the year. People could gather for seasonal events, accept special rules, and later disperse into groups with different expectations.

The authors developed this argument in their earlier research paper, [“Farewell to the ‘Childhood of Man’”](https://davidgraeber.org/wp-content/uploads/Farewell.pdf). They revisit accounts of the Nambikwara in Brazil. Leadership during mobile foraging involved initiative and guidance. In settled village life, it involved more mediation and diplomacy. The chief's role changed with the social setting.

They use such comparisons to reconsider Ice Age archaeology. Rich graves and monumental gatherings show exceptional distinctions and organized labor. These finds do not, by themselves, prove that hereditary rulers commanded everyone all year.

The argument concerns reversible authority. A group might allow command for a particular activity without making obedience a permanent duty. The archaeological extension remains an interpretation, since objects and buildings rarely reveal exactly how people understood their obligations.

## Indigenous people helped shape the argument about freedom

The book also challenges the treatment of Indigenous societies as examples for European thinkers to classify. Indigenous people were critics and political thinkers themselves.

Kondiaronk, a Wendat statesman, is central to this argument. Dialogues published by the French writer Lahontan presented an Indigenous critic questioning European religion, punishment, money, and obedience. Graeber's earlier essay, [“The Wisdom of Kandiaronk”](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/david-graeber-the-wisdom-of-kandiaronk), develops the case that these discussions reflected real intellectual encounters.

The authors argue that such criticism helped shape European debates about equality. They also suggest that theories ranking societies by stages of development helped Europeans dismiss the criticism. Indigenous freedom could then be described as an immature condition that civilized people had necessarily outgrown.

The sources need caution. Lahontan wrote literary dialogues, not transcripts. The authors acknowledge embellishment while arguing against treating the Indigenous contribution as imaginary. Their broader point is that political ideas developed through encounters between societies, as well as within Europe.

## Farming was a range of choices

Cultivating plants did not instantly turn people into full-time farmers. Communities could combine gardens, hunting, fishing, gathering, and animal keeping. They could increase one activity without abandoning the others.

In their essay [“How to Change the Course of Human History”](https://davidgraeber.org/wp-content/uploads/How-to-change-the-course-of-human-history.pdf), Graeber and Wengrow describe early cultivation as a prolonged, uneven process. They discuss people trying farming without accepting every commitment associated with it. The book develops this flexibility through the idea of an **ecology of freedom**.

Keeping several food sources could preserve options. Dependence on one crop, one territory, or one demanding work schedule could reduce them. The authors therefore question the idea of agriculture as a single trap into which humanity fell.

This does not mean every community had unlimited choice. Climate, available species, and neighboring populations mattered. The claim is narrower: knowing that people cultivated grain does not tell us who owned land, who controlled work, or whether anyone could compel obedience.

## Neighbors could choose sharply different lives

Similar ways of obtaining food did not produce identical institutions. The book compares Indigenous societies of the Pacific Northwest with societies farther south in California. It emphasizes contrasts in slavery, aristocratic display, work, and wealth.

The authors suggest that communities sometimes defined themselves through refusing neighboring practices. They use **schismogenesis** for this process of becoming different through interaction. In everyday terms, people can build an identity around a decision that they will not live as their neighbors do. The [scholarly exchange on the book](https://davidgraeber.org/press/critical-exchange-the-dawn-of-everything/) examines this argument in detail.

The implication is that geography and food production alone cannot explain a political culture. Relations with other societies also shape its choices.

## Cities did not all follow kings

Large settlements are often assumed to require centralized command. Graeber and Wengrow ask readers to look for evidence of that command rather than infer it from size.

Teotihuacan, in present-day Mexico, is a major example. The authors interpret its changing development as a shift away from monumental elite projects toward broad provision of substantial housing. In his [interview with *In These Times*](https://inthesetimes.com/article/david-graeber-book-history-interview), Wengrow describes apartment compounds with courtyards, drainage, and decorated walls. Resources supported an unusually widespread standard of urban housing.

There were differences in wealth. The claim is not that every resident lived identically. It is that a large, complex city could organize shared provision without displaying the familiar pattern of one dominant royal household.

Housing remains are direct evidence. The precise political process behind them is harder to recover. The book reads the change as conscious social reorganization. Readers should keep that interpretation distinct from the surviving buildings. A city can challenge assumptions about kingship without proving that its government resembled a modern democracy.

## Separate the powers bundled into the state

The authors resist searching for one universal origin of the state. They distinguish three bases of domination:

- **Control of violence:** the capacity to threaten, injure, or punish.
- **Control of information:** privileged access to knowledge, records, and administration.
- **Personal charisma:** the capacity to attract followers and win contests for influence.

These powers need not arise together. Administration can exist without a monarch. A celebrated leader need not control an extensive bureaucracy. Their later combination requires historical explanation.

Wengrow clarifies this approach in [“On Historical Materialism and The Dawn of Everything”](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10192232/2/Wengrow_Historical_Materialism_DoE.pdf). The purpose is to compare specific forms of power rather than assume that cities, record keeping, and sovereignty form one inevitable package.

## Three freedoms depend on other people

Against these forms of domination, the book identifies three basic freedoms: to leave, to disobey, and to create or change social arrangements.

These are social capacities. Leaving is meaningful when another community will receive you. Refusing an order is meaningful when refusal does not bring violence or exclusion. Changing institutions requires people who can act together.

In his [*Protean* interview](https://proteanmag.com/2022/03/28/primordial-freedoms-an-interview-with-david-wengrow/), Wengrow stresses the care behind these freedoms. Networks of hospitality could make movement possible. A culture of debate could require leaders to persuade rather than command. Freedom depended on obligations to others, as well as room for individual action.

This explains the book's concern with becoming stuck. The problem is not simply unequal possessions. It is losing effective ways to leave domination, refuse it, or reorganize the relationships that sustain it.

## What readers can use—and what remains unresolved

A useful application is to question claims that an institution is unavoidable. Ask which problem it solves, who gains authority through it, and what alternatives the historical evidence actually excludes. This is an application of the book's approach, not a reform program supplied by the authors.

Consider a workplace that calls permanent managerial control necessary because its projects are complex. The book encourages separating coordination from unrestricted command. It does not establish which structure that workplace should adopt. Scale creates real problems; it does not answer every question about power in advance.

The book's reach also creates limits. In [“Against Method”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/720603), archaeologist Ian Morris praises its research while questioning whether its examples defeat broader explanations of social development. Showing variation does not establish that population, technology, and resources have no patterned effects. He also warns that tentative interpretations sometimes become confident claims.

The scholarly exchange linked above raises another problem: ancient alternatives do not fully explain why modern bureaucratic states became dominant. Demonstrating that other arrangements existed is different from explaining how to sustain them today.

The book is most useful as a challenge to historical inevitability. Its examples widen the range of institutions that deserve serious examination. They leave readers with a demanding question: what material support and shared commitments would make the freedom to change society real?
