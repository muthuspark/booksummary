# How a trading company acquired the power to rule

The conquest of India is often told as the expansion of a European nation. William Dalrymple changes the starting point. In *The Anarchy*, the main institution is a commercial company. Its pursuit of revenue helped turn merchants into rulers and millions of people into subjects of a business.

Published in 2019, the book follows the East India Company's rise against the breakdown of Mughal power. Its central argument concerns the danger of joining private profit to public authority. A corporation that can collect taxes and wage war can impose costs on people who have no control over its decisions. The [author's book page](https://williamdalrymple.com/books/the-anarchy) establishes this focus on corporate conquest.

Dalrymple builds the argument through people, political bargains, and military crises. The result also challenges the idea that British victory was inevitable.

## A wealthy empire became a field of competing powers

The early Company entered an established commercial world. It wanted access to Indian goods and markets. It did not begin with the strength to dictate terms to the Mughal Empire.

The balance changed as imperial authority weakened. Dalrymple gives particular weight to Nadir Shah's sack of Delhi in 1739. The attack devastated the imperial capital and exposed the weakness of its protection. Regional rulers and military leaders then competed over resources and authority.

Yet decline at the center did not make every province equally unstable. Bengal remained a major source of wealth. Its bankers and officials already knew how to collect, move, and lend large revenues. The Company's opportunity lay within this existing political and financial system. These contrasts appear in the [opening material supplied by Waterstones](https://cdn.waterstones.com/special/pdf/9781408864395.pdf).

The title therefore requires care. Disorder helped create opportunities for conquest, but India was not an empty space awaiting government. It contained capable institutions and rival projects for rule.

## Plassey joined a local political plot to Company force

The Battle of Plassey in 1757 was a turning point. Dalrymple's account gives betrayal and financial interests a central role. Robert Clive's success depended on a conspiracy against Bengal's nawab, Siraj ud-Daula. Mir Jafar and the Jagat Seth banking house were crucial participants.

This was not simply a contest between two united national sides. Indian actors pursued their own security, wealth, and political ambitions. Some saw the Company as a useful partner against an existing ruler. Thomas Gidney's [review for LSE](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/103998/1/lsereviewofbooks_2020_01_14_book_review_the_anarchy_the_relentless.pdf) highlights these private alliances as a strength of the book.

Victory brought enormous payments to the Company and its servants. More fundamentally, it made the Company a kingmaker. Mir Jafar's dependence weakened the authority of the government he nominally led. Dalrymple's [authorized Plassey excerpt](https://scroll.in/article/934753/null) describes a transfer of wealth that also changed the balance of political power.

The consequences exceeded the immediate bargain. A partner invited to help replace a ruler could acquire enough leverage to dominate the replacement.

## Buxar made tax revenue the foundation of expansion

Plassey did not settle the struggle. The Company's victory at Buxar in 1764 defeated a coalition that included the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II. In the following year, the Company obtained the **Diwani**: the right to collect the revenues of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa. [UK Parliament's historical account](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/legislativescrutiny/parliament-and-empire/parliament-and-the-american-colonies-before-1765/parliament-and-the-east-india-company/) identifies these victories as the basis of its territorial power.

The distinction between a payment and a continuing revenue stream matters. Treasure can finance one expedition. Recurring taxes can maintain troops and support further campaigns. The Company could also use Indian revenues to buy Indian goods for export. It increasingly commanded the resources that had once required commercial negotiation.

Dalrymple explains this change in his [interview with *Travels Through Time*](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5d39be915804eb00017ab307/t/602ccea4d46da53240354cd5/1613549224820/William%2BDalyrmple%2B-%2BEast%2BIndia%2BCompany%2B%281764%29.pdf). Control of revenue linked commerce to conquest. Taxes sustained military power; military power protected and enlarged the revenue base.

Read this as a reinforcing relationship, rather than a guarantee of success. Armies were expensive, and possession of a rich province did not remove the danger of financial crisis.

## Indian soldiers and credit made conquest possible

The Company's armies grew from small forces guarding trading establishments. They expanded as Anglo-French competition spread into India. Indian soldiers, often called sepoys, supplied much of their manpower. British state troops also served alongside them. The [National Army Museum's account](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/armies-east-india-company) shows how far these forces developed beyond their original commercial purpose.

Dalrymple also questions explanations based on permanent European technical superiority. In his [interview with Manu S Pillai](https://manuspillai.com/scrolldalrympleinterview/), he stresses that later Indian armies adopted comparable weapons and training. Access to credit and the division of opposing alliances became decisive advantages.

Bankers could lend to a predatory institution if they expected it to repay reliably. That preference helped the Company finance war. Reliability toward creditors could coexist with violence toward subjects.

This is one of the book's most useful distinctions. Commercial trustworthiness is a relationship with particular partners. It does not establish that an institution serves everyone affected by its actions.

## Extraction exposed the failure of accountability

The Bengal famine of 1770 reveals the human stakes of revenue collection. Dalrymple connects the catastrophe to the Company's extraction of wealth and inadequate response to suffering. Crop failure became a test of what the new rulers considered their responsibilities. He discusses the connection between conquest, extraction, and famine in the *Travels Through Time* interview linked above.

The argument is stronger when kept specific. Drought and harvest failure mattered. They do not settle the separate questions of tax pressure, relief, and whose needs governed official choices. A precise death toll is unnecessary to explain that institutional failure.

Nor did Company rule produce secure finances. A financial crisis in 1772 brought British government intervention. The Regulating Act of 1773 and India Act of 1784 increased political oversight, as Parliament's account explains. Private expansion had created obligations that the state could no longer ignore.

Oversight did not mean an end to conquest. The issue became how Britain would supervise and share power with an institution that already ruled territory.

## Shah Alam gives the transfer of power a human center

Shah Alam II connects the narrative's changing cast. He lived through the destruction of Mughal security and the Company's entry into Delhi in 1803. Dalrymple explains in the Pillai interview that the emperor gave the larger story its continuity.

The emperor's position also separates prestige from force. His authority retained value even when he could not command the strongest army. Rival powers sought Mughal recognition or protection of the court while pursuing their own interests.

The later campaigns widened Company dominance. Tipu Sultan died when Seringapatam fell in 1799. War against the Marathas followed, and Company troops took Delhi in 1803. The National Army Museum places these campaigns within the broader expansion of Company armies.

The Delhi ending makes the transfer of imperial authority visible. It does not mean every Indian power had been conquered. Further wars lay ahead. Treating 1803 as an endpoint is a narrative choice, rather than the completion of British control everywhere.

## What the corporate argument explains—and where it needs limits

Dalrymple draws a warning for the present from this history. In his [2019 *TIME* interview](https://time.com/5716016/william-dalrymple-british-east-india-company/), he also describes the Company as an institution that changed over time. It evolved toward a partnership with the state. Calling it a corporation should not hide those changing political relationships.

Its leaders did not always share one objective. Directors could resist military spending while commanders sought personal rewards from victory. The Pillai interview makes this conflict explicit. Conquest could continue even when particular wars did not serve the directors' preferred commercial strategy.

There are limits to the book's viewpoint. Gidney notes that its focus on prominent individuals gives less space to social movements and to other successor powers. The title's disorder can also obscure the new institutions that regional states were building. Readers should distinguish Mughal decline from a uniform collapse of Indian political life.

My practical reading is to ask three questions when business and government become closely joined: Who can authorize force? Who receives the returns? Who bears the losses? These questions follow from the history; they are not a formal framework offered by Dalrymple. Modern companies require evidence of their own. The historical warning is about the arrangements that let private interests command public power.
